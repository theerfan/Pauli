# 1. Classical Field Theory

**Source:** Tong, *Quantum Field Theory*, §1–§1.1 through (1.6) · pages 7–8 (extract `page-013`–`page-014`)

Quantum field theory is quantum mechanics applied to *fields*. Before we quantize anything, we need the classical theory of those fields: what they are, how they move, and how that motion is encoded in a Lagrangian. Tong covers only the minimum needed to start the quantum theory, and comes back to classical fields later when new ideas are required.

This note stops at the Euler–Lagrange equation for a field, (1.6). The Klein–Gordon example that follows in §1.1.1 is the next chunk.

---

## What you should take from this section

- A **field** is a degree of freedom at every point of space. Position $\vec{x}$ is a *label*, not a dynamical variable.
- Field theory therefore has **infinitely many degrees of freedom**.
- Dynamics still come from a Lagrangian, but the Lagrangian is an integral of a local **Lagrangian density** $\mathcal{L}$.
- The **action** is a spacetime integral of $\mathcal{L}$. Stationarity of the action gives the **Euler–Lagrange equations** for the fields.

---

## 1.1 The Dynamics of Fields

### Particle mechanics, in one sentence

A classical particle (or a finite collection of them) is described by a finite list of generalized coordinates $q_a(t)$, $a = 1,\dots,N$. Those $q_a$ are the dynamical variables: they evolve in time, and Newton's laws (or the Euler–Lagrange equations) tell you how.

The particle's position is one of those dynamical variables. If the particle is at $\vec{x}(t)$, then $\vec{x}$ itself is what the equations of motion are for.

### What a field is

A **field** is a quantity defined at every point of space and time:

$$
\phi_a(\vec{x}, t) \tag{1.1}
$$

Both $a$ *and* $\vec{x}$ are labels:

| Label | What it indexes |
|---|---|
| $a$ | which field (or which component of a multi-component field) |
| $\vec{x}$ | which point in space |

Time $t$ is the evolution parameter, just as in particle mechanics. The thing that evolves is the whole *configuration* $\phi_a(\,\cdot\,, t)$: a function of space, at each instant.

**The conceptual switch.** In particle mechanics, position is a dynamical variable. In field theory, position has been demoted to a mere label. You do not ask "where is the field?"; you ask "what is the value of the field *here*?".

A useful picture is a guitar string. The dynamical object is the displacement of the string at every point $x$ along it. The coordinate $x$ names a bit of string; it is not the position of a particle travelling along the string. The electromagnetic field in empty space is the same idea in three dimensions: at each $\vec{x}$ there is a pair of vectors $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{B}$, and those values change with time.

### Infinite degrees of freedom

Because there is (at least) one independent value of $\phi$ at each point $\vec{x}$, a field is a system with **infinitely many degrees of freedom**.

If that sounds formal, discretize space into a lattice of $N$ points. Then $\phi(\vec{x}_i, t)$ is just $N$ coupled coordinates, like $N$ particles. Continuum field theory is the $N \to \infty$ limit, with the lattice spacing sent to zero. QFT will spend a lot of time arguing with that limit (ultraviolet divergences are, in part, the continuum trying to remember infinitely many degrees of freedom at short distance). For now: the classical theory is already infinite-dimensional.

The index $a$ can itself run over several values — a scalar has one component, $\vec{E}$ has three, the 4-potential $A^\mu$ has four — but that is a *finite* extra multiplicity on top of the continuum of points $\vec{x}$.

## An example: the electromagnetic field

The most familiar classical fields are the electric and magnetic fields $\vec{E}(\vec{x}, t)$ and $\vec{B}(\vec{x}, t)$. Each is a spatial 3-vector, so together they are six functions of spacetime.

Maxwell's theory is more tightly organized than that. $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{B}$ can be packaged as the derivatives of a single **4-component field**, the 4-potential

$$
A^\mu(\vec{x}, t) = (\phi, \vec{A}), \qquad \mu = 0,1,2,3.
$$

The index $\mu$ tells you this is a vector in *spacetime*, not just in space. (Do not confuse this $\phi$ — the electrostatic potential, $A^0$ — with the generic scalar field $\phi_a$ of (1.1). Tong uses $\phi$ for both.)

In terms of the potential,

$$
\vec{E} = -\nabla\phi - \frac{\partial\vec{A}}{\partial t}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\vec{B} = \nabla\times\vec{A}. \tag{1.2}
$$

Two of Maxwell's equations then hold **identically**, before any dynamics:

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec{B} = 0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t} = -\nabla\times\vec{E}.
$$

(Tong writes $d\vec{B}/dt$; he means the partial $\partial\vec{B}/\partial t$.)

### Why they are identities

These are not extra physical laws imposed on $A^\mu$. They are automatic consequences of (1.2) and of vector calculus:

$$
\nabla\cdot\vec{B}
= \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec{A})
= 0,
$$

because the divergence of a curl vanishes. For Faraday's law,

$$
\nabla\times\vec{E}
= \nabla\times\left(-\nabla\phi - \frac{\partial\vec{A}}{\partial t}\right)
= -\nabla\times\nabla\phi - \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\vec{A})
= -\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t},
$$

using that the curl of a gradient vanishes, and that $\nabla$ and $\partial/\partial t$ commute. Rearranging gives $\partial\vec{B}/\partial t = -\nabla\times\vec{E}$.

The other two Maxwell equations (Gauss's law and Ampère–Maxwell) are *not* identities. They are the actual equations of motion of $A^\mu$, and they come from a Lagrangian, the same way (1.6) will produce equations of motion for a generic field.

Writing electromagnetism in terms of $A^\mu$ also makes Lorentz covariance visible: $A^\mu$ is a 4-vector, and $\vec{E}$, $\vec{B}$ mix into each other under boosts. That is the sense in which this is "a more sophisticated treatment."

## The Lagrangian

### Particle mechanics, again

The dynamics of $q_a(t)$ come from a Lagrangian $L(q, \dot{q})$ (no $\ddot{q}$). The action is $S = \int dt\, L$, and $\delta S = 0$ produces the Euler–Lagrange equations.

### Field theory: integrate a density

A field has a value at every point of space, so the natural Lagrangian at time $t$ is an *integral over space* of a local function of the field and its derivatives:

$$
L(t) = \int d^3x\; \mathcal{L}(\phi_a, \partial_\mu\phi_a). \tag{1.3}
$$

$\mathcal{L}$ is the **Lagrangian density**. Everyone just calls it "the Lagrangian." Tong is explicit about the abuse of language; we will follow it, but keep the distinction in mind: $L$ is a number (at each $t$), $\mathcal{L}$ is a function of spacetime.

Why a *density*? Because the total "stuff" that generates the dynamics is the sum of contributions from every point. Locality — $\mathcal{L}$ at $\vec{x}$ depending only on $\phi$ and its derivatives *at $\vec{x}$* — is an extra physical assumption, and a strong one. It is why influences propagate at finite speed, and why the Euler–Lagrange equation will be a *partial* differential equation rather than an integro-differential one.

The arguments of $\mathcal{L}$ are written $\phi_a$ and $\partial_\mu\phi_a$. The derivative

$$
\partial_\mu \equiv \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu}
$$

collects the time derivative and the spatial gradient into one 4-vector ($\mu = 0,1,2,3$). Tong also writes this in 3-vector language: $\mathcal{L}$ is a function of $\phi(\vec{x},t)$, $\dot{\phi}(\vec{x},t)$, and $\nabla\phi(\vec{x},t)$. Same data.

### The action

Integrate $L(t)$ in time, and the two integrals become a single integral over spacetime:

$$
S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} dt \int d^3x\; \mathcal{L}
= \int d^4x\; \mathcal{L}. \tag{1.4}
$$

This already looks relativistic: $S$ is a scalar built by integrating a density over $d^4x$. Lorentz invariance, when we impose it, will be a restriction on $\mathcal{L}$ (it should be a Lorentz scalar), not a change in the *shape* of (1.4).

### What $\mathcal{L}$ is allowed to depend on

Tong restricts the class of Lagrangians in three ways. Each restriction has a reason.

**1. No second time derivatives.**
In particle mechanics, $L$ depends on $q$ and $\dot{q}$, not $\ddot{q}$. Likewise, $\mathcal{L}$ depends on $\phi$ and $\dot{\phi}$, not $\ddot{\phi}$.

Higher time derivatives are not forbidden by mathematics, but they are poisonous as physics: a Lagrangian depending on $\ddot{q}$ (Ostrogradsky's theorem) typically has an energy unbounded below, and the Cauchy problem needs extra initial data. We stay with first-order time derivatives.

**2. No higher spatial derivatives, with Lorentz invariance in mind.**
In principle $\mathcal{L}$ could depend on $\nabla\phi$, $\nabla^2\phi$, $\nabla^3\phi$, and so on. Tong keeps only $\nabla\phi$.

The Lorentz-invariant reason is this. Time and space are supposed to sit in one 4-vector $\partial_\mu\phi$. If you include a second *spatial* derivative $\nabla^2\phi$, Lorentz invariance wants the second *time* derivative $\ddot{\phi}$ as well (they combine into $\partial_\mu\partial^\mu\phi$). We already refused $\ddot{\phi}$, so we refuse $\nabla^2\phi$ as an independent argument of $\mathcal{L}$. First derivatives $\partial_\mu\phi$ are the Lorentz-covariant analogue of $(q, \dot{q})$.

(Terms like $(\nabla\phi)^2$ *are* allowed: those are products of first derivatives, not second derivatives of $\phi$.)

**3. No explicit $x^\mu$ dependence.**
$\mathcal{L}$ is not allowed to depend on $x^\mu$ except through $\phi$ and its derivatives. A term like $e^{-x^2}\phi^2$, or a background potential $V(\vec{x})$, would break spacetime translation invariance. Through Noether's theorem (coming later), that would mean energy or momentum is not conserved. All the systems in this course are translation-invariant, so $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}(\phi_a, \partial_\mu\phi_a)$ only.

## The principle of least action

The equations of motion are the condition that $S$ be stationary under variations of the field that keep the endpoints fixed:

$$
\delta S = 0.
$$

"Least" is traditional; what we actually use is a *stationary* point. The derivation is the field-theory twin of the one from classical mechanics, with one extra feature: we have to integrate by parts in *all four* spacetime directions, not just in time.

### Step 1: vary $\mathcal{L}$

Let $\phi_a \to \phi_a + \delta\phi_a$, with $\delta\phi_a$ a small deformation of the field. Then

$$
\delta S
= \int d^4x
\left[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi_a}\,\delta\phi_a
+ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}\,\delta(\partial_\mu\phi_a)
\right].
$$

Two notational points:

- Repeated $\mu$ is summed (Einstein convention), $\mu = 0,1,2,3$. Repeated $a$ is also summed if several field components are present.
- $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)$ treats $\partial_\mu\phi_a$ as an independent argument of $\mathcal{L}$, the same way $\partial L/\partial\dot{q}$ treats $\dot{q}$ as independent of $q$.

### Step 2: derivatives commute with the variation

The variation of a derivative is the derivative of the variation:

$$
\delta(\partial_\mu\phi_a) = \partial_\mu(\delta\phi_a).
$$

That is because $\delta$ changes the *function* $\phi_a$, while $\partial_\mu$ differentiates with respect to the *label* $x^\mu$. The two operations act on different things, so they commute. This is the step that lets us integrate by parts.

### Step 3: integrate by parts

Use the product rule in reverse:

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}\,\partial_\mu(\delta\phi_a)
=
\partial_\mu\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}\,\delta\phi_a
\right)
-
\left(
\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}
\right)\delta\phi_a.
$$

Substitute back:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta S
&= \int d^4x
\left[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi_a}\,\delta\phi_a
+ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}\,\delta(\partial_\mu\phi_a)
\right] \\
&= \int d^4x
\left[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi_a}
- \partial_\mu\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}\right)
\right]\delta\phi_a
+
\partial_\mu\left(
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}\,\delta\phi_a
\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{1.5}
$$

(The last term is inside the integral; Tong's layout makes that easy to miss.)

### Step 4: the surface term vanishes

The last term is a **total divergence**. By the divergence theorem it becomes an integral over the boundary of the spacetime region.

That boundary has two kinds of face:

- **Constant-time slices** $t = t_1$ and $t = t_2$. We keep the endpoints fixed, which means
  $$
  \delta\phi_a(\vec{x}, t_1) = \delta\phi_a(\vec{x}, t_2) = 0.
  $$
  The variation is zero all over those two faces, so they contribute nothing.
- **Spatial infinity** $|\vec{x}|\to\infty$. We take $\delta\phi_a(\vec{x}, t)$ to decay there (or work in a large box with the field fixed on the walls). Then the spatial face contributes nothing either.

So for every variation we are allowed to consider, the surface term is zero, and

$$
\delta S
= \int d^4x
\left[
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi_a}
- \partial_\mu\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}\right)
\right]\delta\phi_a.
$$

### Step 5: $\delta S = 0$ for *all* such $\delta\phi_a$

The remaining integrand is (coefficient) $\times$ $\delta\phi_a$. If this integral vanishes for *every* compactly-supported $\delta\phi_a$ with fixed endpoints, the coefficient itself must vanish at every point:

$$
\partial_\mu\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}\right)
- \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi_a}
= 0. \tag{1.6}
$$

These are the **Euler–Lagrange equations** for the fields $\phi_a$. There is one equation for each component $a$.

If the "for all variations $\Rightarrow$ coefficient is zero" step feels thin: suppose the coefficient were nonzero at some interior point. By continuity it would be nonzero in a small neighbourhood. Choose $\delta\phi_a$ to be a bump supported in that neighbourhood, with the same sign as the coefficient. Then $\delta S \neq 0$, a contradiction. This is the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations.

## Reading (1.6)

Compare the particle and field versions side by side:

| | Particles | Fields |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamical variables | $q_a(t)$ | $\phi_a(\vec{x}, t)$ |
| Labels | $a$ | $a$ *and* $\vec{x}$ |
| Lagrangian | $L(q, \dot{q})$ | $L = \int d^3x\,\mathcal{L}(\phi, \partial_\mu\phi)$ |
| Action | $\int dt\, L$ | $\int d^4x\,\mathcal{L}$ |
| Euler–Lagrange | $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{q}_a}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_a} = 0$ | $\displaystyle\partial_\mu\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi_a)}\right) - \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi_a} = 0$ |

The field equation is the particle equation with two replacements:

1. $L \to \mathcal{L}$ and $q_a \to \phi_a$.
2. $\frac{d}{dt}$ → $\partial_\mu$, summing over all spacetime directions.

The spatial derivatives in $\mathcal{L}$ are on the same footing as the time derivative. That is the whole point of writing $\partial_\mu\phi_a$ as a single object.

Expanded in $3+1$ language, (1.6) is

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\phi}_a}\right)
+ \partial_i\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_i\phi_a)}\right)
- \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi_a}
= 0,
$$

with $i = 1,2,3$. The middle term is new relative to particle mechanics: it is how spatial gradients of the field contribute to the equations of motion. For a free scalar it will become $-\nabla^2\phi$, i.e. the wave-equation piece.

## Things that are easy to get wrong

**Position is not an operator, and not a coordinate of a particle.**
$\vec{x}$ labels a point of space. When we quantize, the field $\phi(\vec{x})$ at each $\vec{x}$ will become an operator. $\vec{x}$ itself will not.

**$\phi$ in $A^\mu = (\phi, \vec{A})$ is not the scalar field $\phi_a$.**
Electrostatic potential vs. generic field. Same letter, different objects.

**$\mathcal{L}$ vs. $L$.**
$L(t)$ is the integral of $\mathcal{L}$ over space. Euler–Lagrange for fields is written in terms of $\mathcal{L}$, not $L$. If you try to copy the particle formula using $L$ and treating each $\phi(\vec{x})$ as a separate $q_{\vec{x}}$, you *can* make that work (the continuum limit of a lattice of coupled oscillators), but you have to remember a factor of the volume element. The density language does that bookkeeping automatically.

**$\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)$ treats $\partial_\mu\phi$ as an independent slot.**
When you differentiate $\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12\dot{\phi}^2 - \tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2 - \cdots$ with respect to $\dot{\phi}$, you do *not* then differentiate the $\dot{\phi}$ that is hiding inside some other term via the chain rule. The two arguments $\phi$ and $\partial_\mu\phi$ are independent for the purpose of these partials, exactly as $q$ and $\dot{q}$ are.

**The last term of (1.5) is still under the $\int d^4x$.**
It is $\int d^4x\;\partial_\mu(\cdots)$, a surface term, not a leftover local contribution.

**Identities vs. equations of motion, in electromagnetism.**
$\nabla\cdot\vec{B} = 0$ and Faraday follow from writing $\vec{E},\vec{B}$ in terms of $A^\mu$. Gauss and Ampère–Maxwell do not; they are Euler–Lagrange for $A^\mu$ (after gauge fixing / constrained analysis, which Tong will come to).

## Recap

1. Fields $\phi_a(\vec{x}, t)$ assign a value to every point of spacetime. $\vec{x}$ is a label; the system has infinitely many degrees of freedom.
2. The electromagnetic field is the prototype. Its 4-potential $A^\mu$ is a spacetime vector, and two Maxwell equations are identities once $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{B}$ are written as in (1.2).
3. Dynamics come from a Lagrangian density $\mathcal{L}(\phi_a, \partial_\mu\phi_a)$. The action is $S = \int d^4x\,\mathcal{L}$.
4. $\mathcal{L}$ depends on the field and its first derivatives only, and not explicitly on $x^\mu$.
5. Stationarity of $S$, with $\delta\phi$ vanishing at $t_1$, $t_2$ and at spatial infinity, yields the Euler–Lagrange equation (1.6).

Next in Tong is §1.1.1: plug the simplest Lorentz-invariant $\mathcal{L}$ into (1.6) and get the Klein–Gordon equation.
